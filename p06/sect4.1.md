# Section 4.1: Differences in Scope

# Question 1
I think that my experience mostly lines up with the paper. Pretty much every class I've taken so far has had really well-defined assignments. In CS 64 the MIPS labs told you exactly what the function had to do, and you'd just keep submitting to the autograder until all the tests passed. CS 130A was similar, the problem was always clearly stated and there was basically one "right" output. That's the same thing P10 was describing where the tests are written for you and you just make them pass.

Where it's more of a mix is outside of regular classes. During my internship, a lot of the work didn't come with a clear spec at all. Sometimes I'd get a task and part of the job was figuring out what the actual problem even was. Same thing with ACM, where we build websites and dashboards for other student clubs. The clubs usually don't know exactly what they want at the start, and they'll change their minds halfway through, so we end up having to scope things ourselves. That matches what the paper says about people learning this stuff from projects outside of school, not from coursework. This is one of the first classes I've had that feels closer to that, since it's a team project and not just a list of tests to pass.

# Question 2
The main surprise was that requirements in industry are vague and open-ended, while school assignments are narrow and clearly defined (mostly because they have to be easy to grade). P10 said that half the time they don't even know what problem they're solving, which is pretty different from school where the tests are already written.

The other thing was that requirements aren't fixed. There's a lot of back and forth between engineers and PMs about what should actually get built. P19 talked about how PMs can come up with all kinds of ideas, but someone still has to implement them, so you end up debating the best solution for both the product side and the engineering side. That kind of negotiation just doesn't happen in a normal school assignment where the spec is final.

# Question 3
One idea from the paper is P14's suggestion that instructors should change the specs on purpose after students have already started coding. It would probably be pretty annoying in the moment, but it's realistic, since requirements change all the time at work. The paper also points out that project courses were the most helpful for a lot of people. P20 said they helped because of the tight deadlines and having to work with teammates who have different priorities and work ethics. And a lot of the interviewees said side projects outside of school were where they actually learned things like deployment and scale, so doing more of those is another suggestion from the paper.

I also have a few ideas of my own. One is giving assignments with a vague problem statement and no full spec, so students have to write their own requirements first (and maybe get graded on that part too). Another is having students build something for a real "client," like another student, a club, or some campus org. When the person you're building for doesn't know exactly what they want, you're forced to ask questions and scope things down yourself, which is pretty much what I've had to do with ACM. My last idea is having a TA or the instructor act like a PM who asks for features that aren't really realistic, so students get practice pushing back and negotiating, kind of like what P19 described.
